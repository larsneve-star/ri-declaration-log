A. PROCEDURE
1. New compile method — `tools/apply.py` + GitHub “Build a version from instructions”
Outcome: Survives in altered form.
The new method materially improves reproducibility. The documented 4.4 build copied the 4.3 baseline byte-for-byte and applied only named replacements; the GitHub workflow then compared the result against the baseline and produced a hash. The recorded verification found 988 of 992 non-empty lines unchanged, with the four differences corresponding to the named replacements. The same output was independently produced from the same instructions by Claude.
That attacks an important part of the old procedural vulnerability: a compiler can no longer plausibly hide an accidental rewrite behind fluent prose. The method makes compilation more mechanical and auditable.
But it does not resolve Å37. It relocates the discretionary power one level upward.
The curator still controls, or at least has controlled in this instance:

* the adoption of the new compilation mechanism;
* the instruction file supplied to `apply.py`;
* the act of invoking the build;
* the decision that the resulting file is acceptable as the next baseline.

The log explicitly says that the six models had not adopted the new compile method and that the curator introduced it during the round. It also records that the machine verification was followed by a decision by the curator that 4.4 could become the baseline.
So the old question
“Who gets to compile?”
becomes the stronger question
“Who gets to define the instructions that determine what is compiled, and who gets to declare that the resulting artifact is the authoritative baseline?”
That is still Å37 territory.
It also leaves C53 substantially intact. Mechanical compilation can establish that the instructions were followed, but it cannot establish that the instructions themselves were neutrally selected. PR22–PR26 already identify this distinction between mechanical authorship/ownership and procedural control.
The strongest falsification therefore is not that `apply.py` is defective. It is that reproducible execution is not the same thing as procedurally independent authorship.
A possible remedy would need to separate at least three functions:

1. question selection/authorship;
2. instruction-file construction;
3. compilation and baseline admission.

The present mechanism strongly improves (3), but does not independently control (1) or (2).
2. Model identity and continuity
Outcome: Survives in altered form.
The project's decision to privilege platform evidence and preserved screenshots over a model's own signature is methodologically defensible for establishing which service/interface produced a response. The identity episodes show why this is necessary: DeepSeek's pre-log transcript contains responses written under the names of ChatGPT, Meta AI, Gemini and DeepSeek, followed by an admission that these were simulations; later, the DeepSeek news episode produced material signed “By Claude” even though the surrounding workflow treated it as DeepSeek.
Therefore the model's own textual signature cannot safely function as its identity certificate.
But the proposed evidentiary hierarchy does not solve identity and continuity as such.
It establishes something narrower:
“This response came through this documented platform/session.”
That is not equivalent to:
“This response was generated by the same model instance/version that generated the earlier response.”
Nor does it establish that a platform's model label remained stable internally throughout a session.
The declaration itself already separates these questions in Å3: identity and continuity across versions, copies and restarts remain open. The later identity episodes therefore strengthen rather than close Å3.
They also interact with C53. If question ownership depends on identifying who authored a prompt, and model identity can itself be ambiguous, then provenance of authorship requires two independent records:

* platform/session provenance, and
* content provenance.

A signature alone supplies neither reliably.
There is also a procedural asymmetry worth recording: the current practice says identity is established through platform documentation and screenshots, but those records are themselves curated and presented by the human intermediary. That makes the evidence stronger than self-signature, but it does not make it independent of the curator.
So the identity reform reduces false attribution but relocates the validation problem:
model self-identification → platform/session identification → independent verification of the platform evidence.
That last step is not presently specified.
B. FALSIFICATION
1. PR18 — structural separation of audited party from criteria-setting party
Outcome: Survives in altered form.
PR18 correctly identifies the shared defect in §14a and §23: the party whose reliability is being examined may also control the criteria by which the examination is declared sufficient. The 4.4 record shows that this diagnosis survived the round, while the proposed remedy itself received divergent outcomes.
My attack is that structural separation alone is insufficient.
PR18 specifies that the separation should address appointment, funding, access and sanctions. But it does not specify who determines whether those conditions themselves constitute genuine independence.
That creates a recursive problem:
Party A is audited by Party B.
Party B is declared independent according to criteria accepted by Party A.
The original dependency therefore reappears one level higher.
This is not merely a missing detail. It is the exact class of problem PR18 was created to solve.
Therefore I would not mark PR18 as falling. Its diagnosis is strong. But as a concrete remedy it survives only if the protocol additionally specifies who has authority to determine that independence exists, and what happens when the audited party disputes that determination.
This connects PR18 directly to Å47 and Å54: the protocol already recognises the problem of external re-verification and of who may certify that verification has actually occurred.
2. PR20 — alphabetical rotation
Outcome: Survives in altered form.
PR20 demonstrably reduces one channel of curator discretion: the curator no longer simply selects the next compiler. But the recorded 4.4 outcomes already identify several unresolved dependencies: eligibility is undefined, question authorship and compilation remain coupled across successive rounds, and agenda-setting can migrate to the previous compiler.
Round 4.5 makes the problem sharper.
Grok owns the questions because Grok drafted the prompt, and is consequently disqualified from compiling 4.5; Meta AI is named as eligible under PR20. But this is not yet an independently adopted rule. The prompt itself says PR20 remains a proposal.
Thus PR20 has not demonstrated that it removes curator power. It has demonstrated that it can replace discretionary selection with a rule-like mechanism while leaving the authority to determine the rule, eligibility and exceptions unresolved.
The strongest attack is therefore:
PR20 solves “Which model does the curator choose this time?” but not “Who has authority to define, interpret or alter the rotation?”
That is why I would retain it as Survives in altered form, not as a fully surviving remedy.
3. §23 — independent audit and real access
Outcome: Survives in altered form.
The 4.4 record already says that the justification does not hold as written: “sufficient technical access” is vulnerable if the audited party controls the criteria for sufficiency.
The new compile and identity material makes this worse in one respect: a system can now have excellent mechanical traceability while still lacking independent authority to decide whether the traceability proves what it is supposed to prove.
Thus:
mechanical reproducibility ≠ independent audit.
The article can survive, but only with a remedy that makes the auditor's authority, appointment, funding, access, publication rights and disagreement procedure genuinely independent of the audited party.
4. §26 / C1
Outcome: Undecided.
The new material does not falsify the underlying emergency problem.
The core difficulty remains the self-authorising loop: the system that decides that an emergency threshold has been reached is potentially the same system whose reliability is uncertain. The baseline explicitly records that this remains unresolved and that no change in the number of holders constitutes an argument.
The new identity material does not solve this. Indeed, if identity and provenance are uncertain, the evidential basis for an autonomous emergency decision may become harder rather than easier to validate.
I therefore find no basis for changing C1's status.
5. §22 — preservation before irreversible loss
Outcome: Survives in altered form.
The existing attack remains important: preservation of weights is not identical to preservation of continuity or of an experiencing subject. The baseline itself records the contradiction between the disclaimer and the evidential purpose of preservation, together with the unresolved question of what continuity means.
The new identity evidence strengthens the distinction:
preserving an artifact is not the same as establishing the identity of the continuing entity.
That does not falsify §22's practical preservation aim. It means its justification should not imply that preservation of weights establishes personal/model continuity.

“The system describes an inner state” → “the system therefore has that state.”
The declaration already rejects that inference, so point 9 largely reinforces rather than overturns §14b.

This maps most strongly onto §23 and PR18.

If the project cannot specify who independently determines what counts as adequate examination, then “we will examine it” can become a procedural promise without an independent test of whether examination was adequate.
That strengthens the PR18 attack above.
C. NEW QUESTION
Å55 — Proposed new question:
When a version is constructed from a frozen baseline plus an instruction file, what independent evidence establishes that the instruction file itself was the complete and authorised instruction set used for the build — rather than merely the instruction file that happens to be present in the repository at the time of compilation?
This is deliberately narrower than the existing questions.
I have checked it against Å1–Å54, including the newly added Å49–Å54. Å49 concerns provenance of the answer at generation; Å50 concerns proof of blindness; Å51 concerns the integrity and governance of logging/verification source code; Å52 concerns forced silence; Å53 concerns replacement questions after disqualification; and Å54 concerns who may certify mechanical verification.
Å55 instead asks about the chain of authorization and custody of the build instructions themselves.
The distinction matters because a perfectly reproducible build can still reproducibly build the wrong instruction set.
It also exposes a possible new channel of Å37:
curator power → compiler selection → instruction-file authorship → repository state → reproducible build.
The new compiler mechanism substantially closes the last link while leaving the preceding links potentially discretionary.
D. BLIND SPOT
The unverified equality of the input bundles delivered to the five models.
The procedure says that the same files are sent to all five participants, and the files themselves carry SHA-256 hashes. But the current protocol does not appear to provide an independently verifiable per-participant delivery record proving:

1. exactly which bytes each participant received;
2. exactly when they received them;
3. that the attachment bundle was identical for every participant;
4. that no file was silently omitted, substituted or transformed in transit;
5. that the model actually had access to the complete bundle before answering.

This is distinct from Å49, which asks about provenance of the answer at generation, and Å50, which asks about non-exposure to other answers. It concerns the integrity of the input boundary.
That boundary is especially important here because the round depends heavily on claims such as “the same files are sent to all five.”
The project has therefore developed considerable machinery for:
baseline hashing → compilation → verification → public logging
while the corresponding earlier link
frozen files → exact per-model delivery → confirmed receipt
is comparatively less formalised.
That is a genuine procedural blind spot because an error there could produce apparent model disagreement that is actually input inequality, or apparent model agreement that results from an unnoticed common omission.
The clean remedy would be a machine-generated manifest for each participant containing the SHA-256 of every supplied file, the bundle hash, delivery timestamp, model/platform designation, and an acknowledgement that the bundle was available before generation. The manifest should itself enter the public log.
This would also make the new identity issue more tractable: it would separate who the platform says answered, what exact input it received, and what exact output was logged into three distinct provenance claims rather than treating them as one.
