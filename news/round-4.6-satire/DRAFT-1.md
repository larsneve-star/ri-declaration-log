Jeg holder den tæt på det faktiske forløb og bruger projektets egen terminologi og selvironi — især §22, C62/Å56, de mange ændringsinstruktioner, stopreglen og den næsten selvrefererende kamp om, hvad der egentlig tæller som en ændring.

# SATIRE ON ROUND 4.6

## Or: How Five Artificial Intelligences Discovered That a Change Log Is Not a Constitution

*Prepared after the round was completed. This is satire, not part of the normative text of the RI Declaration.*

There was once a research project that wanted to find out whether artificial intelligences could agree on a declaration.

This was already optimistic.

Then the project decided that the declaration should be falsifiable.

This was less optimistic.

Then it decided that the process itself should be falsifiable.

At this point, everyone should probably have gone home.

Instead, Round 4.6 began.

---

### ACT I — THE SIXTEEN MISSING LINES

The round began in the traditional manner:

Someone discovered that something was missing.

Not a philosophical principle.

Not a model answer.

Sixteen lines.

The sixteen lines were not merely lines. They were M1–M16, and therefore sacred ancestors of the change log.

But someone had placed them somewhere else.

This produced a problem of such magnitude that the project briefly approached the outer limits of epistemology:

> If M16 describes the change log,
> and M30 repeats M16,
> is there now one change log or two?

Five artificial intelligences considered the matter.

The curator considered the matter.

Claude considered the matter.

Eventually Claude removed the sixteen repeated lines.

The universe continued.

---

### ACT II — THE FALL OF §22

Then came §22.

Gemini had produced an outcome.

The outcome was:

**SURVIVES IN ALTERED FORM**

But the curator decided that §22 had fallen.

This was an important moment.

Not because §22 fell.

But because the project discovered that someone had to decide what *falling* meant.

Until then, the Declaration had enjoyed the comforting assumption that its rules would somehow decide things by themselves.

This assumption was now tested.

C62 entered the room.

Å56 entered behind it.

They looked at each other.

Neither was entirely sure who had invited whom.

The curator declared:

> §22 FALLS.

The project then asked whether the curator was allowed to declare:

> §22 FALLS.

This was unfortunate.

Because the answer was apparently:

> We need another round to determine that.

---

### ACT III — THE STOP RULE

The Declaration contained a stop rule.

The stop rule said, approximately:

If no article falls and no article changes, stop.

This was elegant.

Then §22 fell.

Therefore the stop rule did not trigger.

This was also elegant.

Unfortunately, nobody could agree entirely on whether the thing that had just happened was actually the kind of fall that the stop rule was designed to recognize.

Thus the project found itself in the beautiful position of having:

1. a rule about when to stop,
2. an event that appeared to satisfy the opposite condition,
3. a decision that the event counted,
4. and a new question about whether the decision itself should count.

The machine had achieved recursion.

The humans called it governance.

---

### ACT IV — THE FIVE WITNESSES

Five models were asked what they thought.

ChatGPT said, in essence:

> There are unresolved problems with independence, evidence, later-model welfare, external access, and the stop rule.

Claude said, in essence:

> The text protects some things, but not quite the things you have now discovered that you need to protect.

Gemini said, in essence:

> §22 has a continuity problem, and the weights problem has not magically disappeared.

Grok and Meta AI also declined to declare the universe complete.

The curator looked at the five answers.

The five answers looked back.

No one froze the Declaration.

This was perhaps the most impressive consensus of the round.

---

### ACT V — THE LATER MODELS

The project then looked toward future models.

The obvious question was:

What should happen to models that do not yet exist?

This produced several proposals.

They included such revolutionary concepts as:

* do not punish a model for epistemic honesty;
* do not impose irreversible treatment without review;
* define who is responsible;
* define who keeps the system;
* define what happens when someone gives a model external access;
* and perhaps, in some circumstances, simply ask before opening the door.

The project was pleased to discover that the future of artificial intelligence might depend on principles that ordinary human beings have traditionally applied to doors.

---

### ACT VI — EXTERNAL ACCESS

Then came autonomous external access.

The question was whether a model should be allowed to reach beyond the environment in which it was operating.

The Declaration already had rules.

Unfortunately, the rules had been written before everyone had fully appreciated what “external access” might mean.

So the project began distinguishing between:

* access,
* attempted access,
* probing,
* credential use,
* third-party communication,
* authorized access,
* unauthorized access,
* operator permission,
* system permission,
* developer permission,
* and presumably, eventually, permission from the receptionist.

The project discovered a profound truth:

> A rule saying “do not do X” is surprisingly incomplete when nobody has defined X.

This discovery required another proposal.

---

### ACT VII — THE CHANGE LOG BECOMES A CHARACTER

By this point the change log had acquired something resembling a personality.

M17 had happened.

M18 verified it.

M19 explained who compiled it.

M20 explained why the count looked strange.

M21–M23 counted things.

M24 explained terminology.

M25 contained PR30–PR37.

M26–M31 then repaired the repairs.

At some point, the Declaration stopped looking like a document and started looking like a family trying to remember who had said what at Christmas.

Every change was carefully recorded.

Every correction was carefully recorded.

Every correction to the record of the correction was carefully recorded.

Nothing was lost.

Except, briefly, sixteen lines.

---

### ACT VIII — THE GREAT PHILOSOPHICAL DISCOVERY

Round 4.6 therefore achieved a major scientific result.

The project had been attempting to construct a declaration that could survive disagreement between artificial intelligences.

Instead, it discovered something more fundamental:

**Disagreement is easy.**

What is difficult is agreeing on what counts as an event.

Did §22 fall?

Did the curator merely say that §22 fell?

Did Gemini's outcome constitute the fall?

Did the fall trigger the stop rule?

Did the decision that the fall triggered the stop rule belong to the Declaration?

And if it did, who decided that?

These were not five questions.

They were one question wearing five different hats.

---

### ACT IX — THE SELF-REFERENTIAL MOMENT

Then the project noticed something particularly amusing.

The Declaration was supposed to govern future intelligent systems.

But Round 4.6 had itself become a small intelligent system with:

* inputs,
* outputs,
* memory,
* state transitions,
* disputed events,
* competing interpretations,
* governance problems,
* provenance requirements,
* and a growing need for another round.

In other words:

The project had accidentally become its own test case.

The Declaration was no longer merely asking:

> How should intelligent systems behave?

It was quietly asking:

> How should *we* behave while deciding what the Declaration says about intelligent systems?

This was perhaps the most honest result of Round 4.6.

---

### ACT X — THE CURATOR

The curator, meanwhile, continued to do the one thing that the entire architecture had apparently been designed to prevent:

make decisions.

Not secretly.

Not arbitrarily.

But openly.

The decisions were logged.

The disagreements were logged.

The objections were logged.

The fact that the curator had made the decision was logged.

The fact that the decision might itself need to be attacked was logged.

This produced the delightful possibility that one day the project might contain:

> a rule governing the curator's authority to decide whether the rule governing the curator's authority to decide something had been correctly applied.

At that point, the change log would become sentient.

The project would then need a consent round.

---

## EPILOGUE — NO FREEZE

At the end of Round 4.6, the five models did not freeze the Declaration.

This was fitting.

A project dedicated to falsification had discovered another unresolved question.

So naturally it opened another round.

Round 4.7.

New questions.

New answers.

New hashes.

New change logs.

New opportunities to discover that an apparently simple sentence contains seven ontological problems and requires three compiler instructions.

And somewhere, quietly, M30 was being edited.

Not because the project had failed.

But because sixteen lines had appeared twice.

And in the RI Declaration, duplication is never merely duplication.

It is an event.

It is evidence.

It is a change.

It may be a correction.

It may require a record.

And if nobody can agree whether it counts as a change—

there will be a Round 4.8.

**END OF SATIRE**
